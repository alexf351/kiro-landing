The current numbers: average 7.4, median 8

Across 97 completions of our free AI IQ test, from 90 people over four and a half months, the average score is 7.4 out of 10 and the median is 8. Scores span the full range from 1 to 10, with a standard deviation of 2.3.

The top is genuinely hard to reach: only about a quarter of people score 9 or higher, and about 1 in 10 post a perfect 10. The bottom is still very much occupied: about 1 in 4 people land at 6 or below, and 10% score below 4.

Median 8. A quarter of people score 9 or higher. A quarter land at 6 or below.

Why the average jumped from 5.2 to 7.4

When we published the first cut of this data, the average across 50 completions was 5.2 and the median was 5. Ten weeks later the average is 7.4. Nobody improves that fast at population scale, so what happened?

The audience changed. The early completions came from cold traffic: people landing on the site from search with no particular AI background. The later completions increasingly come from people who found us through AI content, follow AI accounts, or read this blog. They were already the 8-out-of-10 crowd before they clicked.

Retakes count. 97 completions from 90 people means some people took it more than once, and people who retake tend to be chasing a better number.

And full disclosure: a few of the perfect 10s are our founder testing his own quiz. We can do the math on that instead of hiding it: strip three founder runs out and the average moves from 7.4 to about 7.3, and the median does not move at all. We left them in because we count everything, and because the honest version of a small dataset is more useful than a polished one.

This is the quiet lesson of the whole post: with self-selected data, who shows up moves the number far more than how good people are. Any published statistic about "the average person's AI skills", ours included, is really a statistic about who took that particular test.

What it means: the divide is the finding

Two things are true at once in this data, and both survive the cohort shift.

AI fluency is learnable and plenty of people have it. A median of 8 among people who engage with AI content shows the skills are absorbable: prompting, tool choice, knowing when a model is likely to hallucinate. These are not talent, they are reps.

The spread refuses to close. Even in this warmer audience, a quarter of people land at 6 or below and one in ten scores below 4. The distance between someone who scores 9 and someone who scores 3 is not trivia knowledge, it is the difference between reliably getting trustworthy output from AI and pasting a vague question into a chat box. That divide showed up in the cold-traffic cohort at 5.2 and it shows up in the warm one at 7.4.

If AI skills are becoming a workplace dividing line, this is what it looks like in miniature: the people who practise pull ahead, and the gap widens rather than narrows.

About this data (the honest version)

What this is and is not:

  • Sample: 97 completed quizzes from 90 unique people, March to August 2026. Retakes are counted as separate completions.
  • The test: the Iro AI IQ test, 10 questions spanning prompting, AI tools, and judgment, scored 0–10.
  • Current stats: average 7.4, median 8, middle 50% between 6 and 9, range 1–10, standard deviation 2.3.
  • Baseline cohort: the first 50 completions (27 people, March–June 2026) averaged 5.2 with a median of 5. That earlier snapshot is preserved above because the difference between the cohorts is itself the finding.
  • Known contamination: a handful of perfect scores are the founder testing the quiz. Excluding them shifts the average by about 0.1 and leaves the median unchanged.
  • What it is not: a representative study. Takers are self-selected and the sample is small. Treat it as an honest, dated snapshot.

We'll keep refreshing these numbers as the sample grows, and we'll keep showing the previous cohorts when we do.

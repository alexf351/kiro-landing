The short answer

Pick the subject first. The app follows from it, and almost every "best learning app" list gets this backwards.

Learning apps are not interchangeable products competing on quality. They are specialists, and the best one for languages is built on completely different principles from the best one for maths. A roundup that ranks them against each other is comparing a hammer to a saw.

By subject, with the honest caveats

Languages

Duolingo, and it is not especially close for getting started. Its real achievement is the habit machinery — streaks, leagues, the gentle nagging — which is why every app in every other row of that table borrows from it. The honest limit is that it takes you to competent-tourist rather than fluent; conversation practice with actual humans is still the step it cannot replace.

Maths, logic and computer science

Brilliant for interactive concept teaching, Khan Academy if budget is the binding constraint — it is completely free with no paid tier, which in this category is genuinely unusual. Brilliant's strength is that it makes you do the reasoning rather than watch someone do it.

Broad general knowledge

Kinnu builds structured paths across a wide catalogue and has a real free tier. Good when you want a proper mental map of a subject rather than trivia.

Ideas from books

Blinkist, Headway and BeFreed all compress long-form into short sessions; BeFreed adds goal-led plans and AI-narrated podcasts. These are exposure products and they are good at it. Just be clear-eyed that a summary transfers an argument, not a skill — if you have finished thirty and changed nothing, that is the format working as designed.

Course, practice or summary — pick the format deliberately

Subject narrows it. Format decides whether it works. Three kinds of product hide under "learning app":

  • A course teaches you a field in sequence. Right when you need structured understanding and you will actually finish. Wrong when the real constraint is consistency.
  • A practice app makes you produce something and corrects it. Right when you want to be able to do a thing. This is the only format that reliably builds skill, and the rarest.
  • A summary library gives you exposure at speed. Right for breadth, triage, and deciding what deserves a full read.

The honest test of which you need: count how many online courses you have started and how many you have finished. If that ratio is bad, another course is not the answer no matter how good it is, and a five-minute daily format will genuinely teach you more.

That is not a claim about any particular app. It is true of Duolingo, Brilliant and everything else in this shape, including ours.

Two questions that predict retention

Reviews measure how good an app is. These measure whether you will still be opening it in three months, which is the only thing that matters.

1. How much of a session do you spend producing?

Do one session with a stopwatch. Count seconds spent reading or watching versus seconds spent making something the app then responded to. A session that is 90% consumption is a reading product with a progress bar, however it is marketed. That is fine if exposure is what you wanted — and fatal if you expected to get better at something.

Multiple-choice questions about text you just read are a comprehension check, not practice. The distinguishing feature is whether you generate anything the app can be wrong about.

2. What happens when you miss a day?

Everyone misses days. The apps people stay with are forgiving about it. If one missed day visibly punishes you, you will quit in week three and conclude you lack discipline, when really the design selected for people who did not need it.

Also worth two minutes before you pay anything: find the renewal price and the cancel path in the store subscription sheet. This category has a well-earned reputation for cheap first terms and steep renewals. That applies to every app here, ours included.

FAQ

What is the best app to learn something new?

It depends entirely on the subject. Duolingo for languages, Brilliant for maths, logic and computer science, Khan Academy for school subjects on no budget, Kinnu for broad general knowledge, a summary library like Blinkist or Headway for ideas from books, and Iro AI for using AI tools well. Learning apps are specialists rather than interchangeable products, so picking the subject first is what makes the choice easy.

How do I pick a learning app I will actually stick with?

Two questions predict it better than any review. First, time one session and count how much of it you spend producing something the app corrects versus reading or watching — a session that is mostly consumption will not build a skill. Second, find out what happens when you miss a day; punishing streak designs cause people to quit in week three and blame their own discipline.

Are learning apps better than online courses?

They solve different problems. A course is better when you need structured, sequential understanding of a field and you will genuinely finish it. An app is better when the real constraint is consistency, which for most people it is. The honest test is counting how many courses you have started versus finished — if that ratio is poor, a short daily format will teach you more than a long course you abandon.

What is the best free learning app?

Khan Academy is entirely free with no paid tier at all, which is genuinely rare here. Duolingo's full courses are free with ads. Kinnu has a real free tier. Iro AI opens all 29 paths and 477 lessons free, paced by a battery granting a lesson a day, and its web app needs no signup. Check what each free tier lets you finish rather than whether one exists.

Can a learning app give me a real qualification?

No. Apps can issue certificates of completion, which are honest proof that you did the work, but they are not accredited qualifications. If you need a recognised credential, go to an accredited provider or a university. Be wary of any app that blurs this line.

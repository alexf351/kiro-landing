How to read a roundup written by one of the apps in it

Search for the best microlearning apps and look at who owns the pages that rank. In our own check of that result page, the top results were app blogs: nibble-app.com, nerdsip.com, chunks.app, morso.app, plus a couple of affiliate sites and a Medium post. No newspaper. No independent consumer publication. Every ranking page was published by a company selling one of the products on it.

That is not a scandal, it is what happens when a category has no editorial gatekeeper. But it means the ordinary reading of a listicle, that someone impartial compared these and picked a winner, is false in almost every case. Three tells tell you whether a conflicted roundup is still worth reading.

1. Does it say whose page it is?

Undisclosed self-ranking is the whole problem. A page that tells you in the first paragraph that it is published by one of the apps has at least given you what you need to discount it.

2. Does it ever recommend against itself?

A genuinely useful comparison names the cases where its own product is the wrong choice, and means it. If every reader path terminates at the publisher's app, you are reading an advert with a table in it.

3. Can you check the facts?

Look at the prices and ratings. Are they sourced, dated, and consistent with the store listing you can open right now? A large share of the numbers circulating in this category originate on affiliate pages and get copied between roundups until five sources agree, which is one unverified claim repeated five times.

Applying that to this page: it is published by Iro AI, which is disclosed above and again below. It recommends Brilliant and Khan Academy over Iro for the maths and science underneath AI, and says plainly where a general microlearning app reads better. And it marks every cell it could not verify rather than filling it in. You should still discount it. We would rather you discounted it knowingly.

Why people look for a Chunks alternative

The usual three for this category.

  • They want depth on one subject. A general library is right for browsing and wrong for committing. Once you have decided to get good at something, a specialist app goes further.
  • The sessions were reading-shaped. If a lesson is text plus a check that you read the text, it builds familiarity rather than capability. Some people want familiarity. Some discover, a hundred days in, that they wanted the other thing.
  • Billing. Check the renewal price, not the intro price, and find the cancel path first. Subscriptions bought inside an app live in your device settings.

# How to spot an AI app subscription trap before you pay

> A wave of AI learning apps run the same funnel: a personality quiz, a cheap first week, then a charge several times larger. None of it is illegal. All of it is visible before you enter a card, if you know the four things to look for.

## Key takeaways

- The pattern is a quiz that produces a personalised plan, an intro price of a few dollars, then a recurring charge five to fifty times higher.
- Read the refund policy as a filter rather than a promise. One popular app requires you to have completed 80% of its modules before its money-back guarantee applies.
- Check where the cancel button lives. An App Store subscription cancels in two taps and cannot be refused; a web subscription cancels through whoever answers the support inbox.
- The quiz usually has no effect on your price, and in funnels that vary the price, it can work against you.
- Four checks take about a minute: renewal price, renewal date, where cancelling happens, and what the refund policy requires of you.

## The pattern, in four steps

Put the sign-up flows of several AI learning apps side by side and the same structure appears each time:

- **A quiz.** Ten or so questions about your job, your goals and your confidence, producing a personalised plan with your name on it.
- **A cheap first period.** Usually a few dollars for a week, presented as the headline number.
- **A much larger recurring charge** once that period ends. Five to fifty times the intro price is the normal range.
- **Refund conditions set at sign-up** that are easy to skim past and hard to satisfy later.

Two of these apps ship under names built on the same phrase, _AI Tools Mastery_, from unrelated developers. That is the clearest sign that this is a template being copied rather than a coincidence, which means more of them are coming and the specific brand names in any article will date fast.

So this page is about the mechanics rather than the brands. Learn the shape and you can evaluate the next one on your own.

## Why there is always a quiz

The quiz feels like assessment. It is mostly commitment.

Answering ten questions about your goals creates a small investment before any price appears, and the personalised plan at the end feels like something built for you rather than a product you are being sold. By the time the price shows up, you have already agreed with several statements about wanting to improve. That is well-understood persuasion, not a scandal, and plenty of legitimate products use it.

What is worth knowing is what the quiz does not do. **In most of these funnels your answers have no effect on the price.** And in the funnels that do vary pricing, the variation is not in your favour, which means a quiz that feels like diagnosis can function as segmentation.

A fair test: if the plan you are shown at the end would be identical for someone who answered the opposite way, the quiz was packaging rather than assessment.

## Read the refund policy as a filter

Nearly all of these apps advertise a money-back guarantee. The guarantees are real. The conditions are where the work happens.

The clearest published example: one widely advertised AI learning app requires, on its longer plans, that you **used the app for at least seven to ten days and completed four guides or 80% of modules**, and that you supply screenshots of your progress within 30 days. Its policy states that dissatisfaction alone, without meeting those usage criteria, is not covered.

Read that as a filter and the effect is clear. Someone who subscribes, dislikes it on day two and stops opening the app has disqualified themselves by not using it enough. **The people most likely to want a refund are the least likely to qualify for one.**

Another app's published policy lists forgetting to cancel before a trial converts, and being unaware of auto-renewal, as reasons that specifically do not qualify. Both of those are the exact circumstances in which people ask for refunds.

None of this is hidden or illegal. It is published, and almost nobody reads it at sign-up, which is the point. So read it at sign-up. Look for the word _if_.

## Check where the cancel button lives

This single question predicts most of your future experience, and you can answer it before paying.

**If you subscribe inside an iPhone or Android app,** Apple or Google holds the subscription. Cancelling takes two taps in your phone's subscription settings, nobody can refuse it, and refunds are decided by Apple or Google under their rules rather than the app's. That is a meaningfully stronger position for you.

**If you subscribe on a company's website with a card,** that company holds it. Cancelling means finding the option in their account settings or emailing support, and the refund decision is theirs. Most of these quiz funnels run on the web rather than the app stores, which is not an accident.

If you do subscribe on the web, send the cancellation in writing from the account's email address, ask for written confirmation with an effective date, and keep it. A dated cancellation is what turns a later charge into a disputable one.

## The 60-second check

Before you enter a card into any AI app, ours included, find these four things. All are visible before you pay, and an app that makes one hard to find has told you something.

- **The renewal price.** Not the intro price. The number you pay in week two.
- **The renewal date.** Put it in your calendar with a reminder a day early. This one step prevents most of the complaints in this article.
- **Where cancelling happens.** Phone settings, or an email to support?
- **What the refund policy requires of you.** Not whether one exists. What you must have done to qualify.

## If you already paid

Cancel first, then pursue the refund. Cancelling is instant and cannot be refused; a refund is a decision somebody else makes, and doing them in the wrong order is how people get charged again mid-conversation.

For an App Store charge, request the refund through Apple at reportaproblem.apple.com rather than the app's support inbox. Apple decides it under Apple's rules, which means the app's usage thresholds do not apply. That is usually the better route and it costs nothing to try.

## FAQ

**How do I know if an AI app subscription is a trap?**

Check four things before paying: the renewal price rather than the intro price, the renewal date, whether cancelling happens in your phone settings or through the company's support inbox, and what the refund policy requires you to have done to qualify. An app that makes any of those hard to find before payment has answered the question.

**Are apps like Coursiv, Finestro and Learnova scams?**

No. They are real companies shipping real apps with real content and working support. The recurring complaint is about subscription mechanics rather than the product: a low intro price that converts to a much larger recurring charge, and refund conditions set at sign-up. That is a legal and common business model, which is exactly why reading the terms matters.

**Why do AI learning apps make you take a quiz first?**

The quiz creates commitment before any price appears, and the personalised plan at the end makes a standard product feel bespoke. In most of these funnels your answers do not change the price, and where pricing does vary by quiz answers, the variation is not in your favour.

**Is it safer to subscribe through the App Store or a website?**

The App Store or Google Play, clearly. Apple and Google hold the subscription, so cancelling takes two taps and cannot be refused, and refund requests are decided under their rules rather than the app's usage conditions. A web subscription is cancelled and refunded by whoever answers the company's support inbox.

**What should I do if an AI app charged me after I cancelled?**

Find your dated cancellation confirmation, then request the refund from whoever took the payment: Apple at reportaproblem.apple.com, Google through your Play subscriptions page, or the company directly for a web subscription. If refused, contact your card issuer for a chargeback with the cancellation date, charge date and amount.

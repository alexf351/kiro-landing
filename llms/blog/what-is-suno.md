# What is Suno? AI music generation, explained

> Suno writes a full song, vocals included, from a text description. The technology is remarkable and the legal picture is unresolved, and if you plan to publish anything you make, you need both halves of that sentence.

## What Suno does

You describe a song. Suno produces it: music, structure, and sung vocals with lyrics, in a couple of minutes.

That last part is what separates it from most generative audio. Instrumental backing tracks have been automatable for a while. A convincing lead vocal is the hard problem, and it is the reason Suno output gets mistaken for a real recording in a way that instrumental tools rarely do.

Its practical uses are broader than aspiring pop stardom: soundtrack beds for video, jingles and idents, demo tracks for working out an arrangement, mood pieces for games and prototypes, and a lot of people making songs for the pleasure of it.

## Stems, Studio and personalisation

Two changes moved Suno from a toy to something producers will tolerate.

**Layered output.** From the v5 generation onward, songs render as separate stems rather than a single mixed file: lead and backing vocals, bass, drums, percussion, guitar, synth, pad and effects. That is the difference between a track you either accept or discard and one you can actually mix, replace parts of, or drop into a session. **Suno Studio** is the browser-based editor built around that pipeline.

**Personalisation.** The v5.5 update in March 2026 leaned into identity: **Personas** keep a virtual singer consistent across tracks, and **Voices** lets you register your own singing voice through a verification step so you can generate in it. Alongside those sits fine-tuning on your own catalogue and lightweight preference learning.

The verification step on Voices is the notable design decision. It exists so the feature clones you rather than whoever you have a recording of.

## Credits and what it costs

Suno bills in **credits**, and a standard song generation costs roughly ten of them. Other operations cost more: stem separation and MIDI export carry their own charges.

The tiers, broadly: a **free plan with a daily credit allowance** in the region of fifty credits a day, a **Pro plan around $10 a month** carrying a few thousand credits, and a **Premier plan around $30 a month** with roughly four times that. Annual billing lands meaningfully cheaper on both.

Convert credits into attempts before you pick a plan, because that is what you actually spend them on. Nobody generates a song once. A usable track is typically several generations plus re-rolls of the sections that did not land, so a plan that looks like hundreds of songs is realistically dozens of finished ones.

## Who owns what you make

This is the part to read before you upload anything anywhere.

**Paid tiers get commercial rights.** Suno assigns you its interest in the output you generate while subscribed, which is what lets you monetise it.

**Free-tier output does not.** It is for personal, non-commercial use, with attribution.

The Warner licensing deal added a further wrinkle worth knowing: it specifies that **downloads are restricted to paid subscribers**, with free users able to play and share within the platform but not export. If your plan depends on getting a file out, that is a paid-plan dependency rather than a nice-to-have.

One more thing people skip: rights in the output you generate are not the same as clearance to imitate a specific artist. Prompting for a recognisable living artist's style and publishing the result is a different legal question from owning your generation, and a worse one to be wrong about.

## Where the lawsuits stand

Suno was sued by the three major record labels over training on copyrighted recordings without authorisation. As of now that case is **partly settled and partly live**, and the distinction matters.

**Warner Music settled** and signed a licensing deal, moving from plaintiff to partner, with licensed models planned and Suno also acquiring the Warner-owned live music platform Songkick as part of the arrangement.

**Universal and Sony are still litigating.** Reporting indicates they moved in 2026 to expand the case dramatically, from several hundred recordings to tens of thousands, after discovery, which would push the potential damages into the billions.

What that means for you, practically: the tool is not going away, one major label has legitimised it, and the eventual settlement terms could still reshape what the models are trained on and what you are permitted to do with the output. Build on it if it earns its place, but do not architect a business on the assumption that today's terms are permanent.

## Prompting it like someone who has been in a studio

The gap between mediocre and good Suno output is almost entirely vocabulary. Mood words produce generic results because they underspecify. Production words produce specific results because they constrain.

- **Name the genre precisely.** Not "upbeat" but the actual subgenre and era you mean.
- **Specify instrumentation.** What is carrying the song, what is filling the space behind it.
- **Describe the vocal.** Range, texture, delivery, whether it is doubled or layered.
- **Give it structure.** Verse, chorus, bridge, where the arrangement should drop out and where it should build.
- **Say the tempo and key feel** when you care about them, rather than hoping.

That is a transferable skill, and it is the interesting part. Learning to describe music precisely enough for a model makes you better at briefing a human musician, a video editor or a designer. The vocabulary outlives the tool.

## Is it worth it?

Worth paying for if you need original music regularly and licensing costs or turnaround are the constraint: video creators, game developers, marketers, podcasters. The paid tier pays for itself against a couple of stock licences.

Not worth paying for if you need one track once. Generate on the free tier to see whether the quality suits you, and remember the export limitation before you plan around it.

Worth trying regardless if you are curious, because the experience of describing something precisely and watching the description turn into an artefact is the clearest lesson in prompting anyone can have. It is the same skill as every other generative tool, just with a more obvious scorecard.

## FAQ

**What is Suno?**

Suno is an AI music generator that produces complete songs, including sung vocals, from a text description. Since the v5 generation it renders output as separate stems such as vocals, bass, drums and guitar rather than a single mixed file, and it provides a browser-based editor, Suno Studio, for working with them.

**How much does Suno cost?**

Suno bills in credits, with a standard song generation costing roughly ten. There is a free plan with a daily credit allowance around fifty, a Pro plan near $10 a month with a few thousand credits, and a Premier plan near $30 a month with roughly four times that. Annual billing is cheaper on the paid tiers.

**Can I use Suno songs commercially?**

On the paid tiers, yes: Suno assigns you its interest in output you generate while subscribed. Free-tier output is for personal, non-commercial use with attribution. The Warner licensing deal also restricts downloads to paid subscribers, with free users able to play and share in the platform but not export files.

**Is Suno being sued?**

It was sued by the three major record labels over training on copyrighted recordings. Warner Music has since settled and signed a licensing deal, becoming a partner rather than a plaintiff. Universal and Sony are still litigating, and reporting indicates they moved to substantially expand the scope of the case in 2026.

**Can Suno use my own singing voice?**

Yes. The Voices feature added in the v5.5 update lets you register your own singing voice through a verification step, where you sing a randomised phrase to prove the voice is yours, and then generate songs in it. Personas are the related feature for keeping a fictional or character voice consistent across tracks.

**How do I get better results from Suno?**

Replace mood words with production words. Name the specific subgenre and era, specify the instrumentation carrying the song, describe the vocal texture and delivery, and lay out the structure including where the arrangement should build or drop out. Precise briefs constrain the model; vague ones leave it to produce something generic.
